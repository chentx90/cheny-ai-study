Frequency and Distribution of Idiopathic Osteosclerosis in Diabetic Patients - 
a Pilot Study in Taiwan

Kuo-Chou Chiu2

Background: Idiopathic osteosclerosis (IOS) refers to lesions of the jaw bone. The present study was undertaken to determine the prevalence of IOS in a group of diabetes mellitus (DM) patients by radiographic evaluation and to investigate 
the relationship between the fi ndings in relation to age, gender, and localization. Methods: Panoramic radiographs and 
clinical information for 832 DM patients were examined to identify IOS. Statistical analyses were performed using chisquare test. Results: In total, 60 IOS lesions were identifi ed in 48 patients. The prevalence of IOS in DM patients was 5.8%. 
All the IOS lesions were detected in the mandible. Twenty-six IOS lesions were associated with root apex and 34 lesions 
were not associated with tooth. IOS occurred more commonly in the fourth (25.0%) and fi fth (28.3%) decades of DM 
patients. In addition, 27 (53.3%) and 21 (35%) IOS lesions were observed in the premolar and molar area of mandible, 
respectively. Conclusions: In DM patients, the frequency of IOS is similar to that in the general population and the relatively prevalent location for IOS is the mandibular premolar region. IOS lesions can arise at any age without sex predilection. Additionally, our fi ndings support the theory that IOS is a variation of normal bone development unrelated to a local 
stimulant.
Key words: diabetes mellitus, mandible, osteosclerosis
INTRODUCTION 
Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a group of metabolic diseases in which a person has high blood sugar, either 
because the body does not produce enough insulin, or 
because cells do not respond to the insulin that is produced. DM could influence the bone through several 
mechanisms, some of which may have contradictory 
effects.1
 Obesity, prevalent in type 2 DM, is strongly associated with higher bone mineral density (BMD) probably through mechanical loading and hormonal factors.2
Hyperinsulinemia may also promote bone formation.3
On the other hand, low levels of insulin associated with 
type 1 DM and the progression of type 2 DM may cause 
reductions in BMD. Hyperglycemia generates a higher 
concentration of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) 
in collagen that may reduce bone strength.4,5 Hypercalciuria associated with glycosuria may induce bone loss.5,6
Insulin-like growth factor-I (IGF-I) is thought to be anabolic for bone and may be lower in DM.7,8 An increase in 
infl ammation and associated cytokines could accelerate 
bone turnover and loss of bone.9 Declining renal function, more prevalent in DM, is associated with lower 
BMD in older women.10 Microvascular complications of 
DM and reduced blood fl ow to bone may contribute to 
bone loss and fragility.11 Therefore, the metabolic abnormalities of DM could affect bone metabolism, structure, 
and mineral density.1
Areas of increased bone production in the jaw are 
frequently identified on dental radiographs. Idiopathic 
osteosclerosis (IOS) refers to a focal area of increased 
radiodensity that is of unknown cause and cannot be attributed to any sequela of a local infection, dysplastic, 
or neoplasia. IOS is asymptomatic, not associated with 
detectable cortical expansion, and is typically detected 
during a routine radiographic examination. Radiographically, IOS presents as round, triangular, or irregularly 
shaped radiopaque (RO) lesions, varying in size from a 
few millimeters to 2 cm in diameter with varying density,

In the literature, IOS is known by a variety of names12-16, 
with the incidence having been surveyed in many studies.17-19
However, their occurrence and distribution in DM patients 
is not clear. In this study, we investigate the frequency 
and distribution of IOS in DM patients.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
1. Patients and radiographic analyses
Panoramic radiographs of 832 DM patients taken from 
2010 to 2011 at the Dental Department of Tri-Service 
General Hospital in Taipei were examined. All fi lms were 
deemed technically acceptable for interpretation. 
2. Diagnostic criteria and classifi cation
IOS was identified as well-defined radiopaque (RO) 
lesions with homogenous, uniformly RO areas, larger 
than 3 mm in size, present in the jaw. They were either 
round or elliptical, with no surrounding radiolucent rim 
(Figure 1). It was not possible to classify these lesions or 
to relate them to a specifi c source such as an infl ammatory reaction or a neoplasm. 
The following RO lesions were specifi cally excluded: 
(1) lesions around teeth with deep caries, large restorations, and/or periodontal disease, (2) identifiable remnants of deciduous or permanent teeth, (3) lesions around 
teeth that serve as abutments, (4) lesions interpreted as 
tori or exostoses, (5) solitary RO lesions in edentulous 
regions, (6) characteristic mixed RO and radiolucent 
areas of periapical cemental dysplasia or other benign fi -
brous-osseous lesions, (7) lesions whose associated teeth 
showed displacement or root resorption, and (8) RO rims 
around anatomic structures, such as the mental foramen.
Two of the authors (YSS and CYC) simultaneously 
reviewed the panoramic radiographs. An RO lesion was 
diagnosed as IOS when both observers came up with the 
same radiographic diagnosis. If there was any disagreement, the lesion was excluded from this survey.
3. Statistical analysis
Demographic data for each patient were recorded to 
investigate the relationship between the fi ndings and age, 
gender, and localization. The observed results were analyzed with SPSS 13.0 (Statistical Package for Social Sciences Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). Data were analyzed 
using the chi-square test, and the results were considered 
to have a signifi cant difference if the signifi cance level 
was less than .05.
　　
RESULTS
1. IOS incidence 
IOS lesions were identifi ed in 48 of the 832 DM patients, giving an overall incidence of 5.8%. Of these 48 
patients, all IOS lesions were observed in the mandible. 
Of the 48 patients with IOS lesions, there were 38 (79%) 
patients with 1 lesion, 8 (17%) patients with 2 lesions; 
and 2 (4%) patients with 3 lesions. Thus, a total of 60 
IOS lesions were found. Some of the IOS lesions were 
associated with tooth (Figure 1A) and those not associated with tooth were apical to and clearly separated from 
the tooth and lamina dura (Figure 1B). Of the 60 IOS lesions, approximately 43.3% (26/60) were associated with 
root apices (tooth-associated), with the remaining 56.7% 
(34/60) of IOS lesions separated from teeth (non toothassociated) 
2. Age and gender distributions of IOS cases
The ages of the subjects ranged from 25 to 76 years 
with a mean of 47.1 years. Most lesions (58.3%) were

DISCUSSION 
In this pilot study, the subjects were DM patients. Our 
results generally support previous fi ndings indicating that 
IOS is predominantly associated with the mandible, occurs frequently in the premolar/molar region but only in a 
minority of patients, and shows no gender predominance. 
The IOS frequency in DM patients in our study is 
5.8%, which is similar to previous fi ndings in the general 
population.17-20 These results also indicated that systemic 
disease such as DM may not be an etiologic factor of 
IOS. The relationship between diabetes and bone disease 
is complex.21 Bone is a tissue that undergoes frequent remodeling and has a large capacity for regeneration. This 
physiologic remodeling is initiated by osteoclasts that resorb bone and is followed by the formation of an equivalent amount of new bone by osteoblasts. Notably, clinical 
data supported that bone formation and bone microarchitectural integrity are altered in the diabetic state.21 Studies in bone histomorphometry in diabetes have generally, but not always, shown a low turnover of bone with 
reduction in bone formation and, to a lesser degree, bone 
resorption. The decrease in bone formation is manifested 
by reduced serum concentrations of osteocalcin, a marker 
of osteoblastic activity. In comparison, resorption markers (such as serum tartrate resistant acid phosphatase and 
urinary hydroxyproline) are increased in some patients, 
perhaps related to alterations in renal function. Evidence 
for changes in bone microstructure22,23 associated with DM 
is found in a number of animal studies.24-26 Moreover, research suggests that hyperglycemia associated with DM 
can alter bone turnover27 and may impair vitamin D and 
calcium metabolism,28 which can also affect the integrity 
of bone for older adults with DM. Other factors that can 
affect bone quality include advanced glycation end products (AGEs), which result in changes to bone collagen.4,29
Therefore, our finding on frequency of IOS in DM 
patients being similar to that in the general population 
supports the theory that IOS lesions are developmental 
variations of normal bone architecture unrelated to systemic diseases such as DM. 
A variety of terms have been used in the literature to 
describe IOS, including dense bone island16,30, enostosis31, focal osteosclerosis14, and periapical osteopetrosis.14
In a review of previous reports19,31-33, the IOS incidence 
varied widely from 2.3% to 31%, and the incidence 
was greater in Oriental populations than in their Western counterparts.32-34 There are a number of reasons for 
these discrepancies in incidence and distribution among 
these surveys. One of the most important reasons is 
the diagnostic criteria used for identifi cation of an IOS 
lesion. For example, we excluded radiopaque (RO) lesions around teeth with deep caries, large restorations, 
or periodontal disease, as well as those associated with 
lesions in the edentulous area. These RO lesions could be 
chronic inflammatory bone lesions such as condensing 
osteitis. Condensing osteitis, also known as chronic focal 
sclerosing osteomyelitis, is the most common periapical 
radiopacity found in adults. In the jaw, IOS lesions can 
be associated with or separated from the teeth. When 
associated with a tooth, it is necessary to distinguish an

Our study showed that the most common site for IOS 
was the premolar regions (53.3%). In a review of previous studies, the more common location for IOS was 
either the molar16,35 or premolar region.18,33 For example, 
Geist examined 2,211 panoramic radiographs and found 
most lesions located in the mandibular premolar.18 McDonnell analyzed 113 dense bone islands in 107 patients, 
and found the mandibular molar region to be the most 
common site of IOS occurrence, followed by the premolar region.16,35 Yonetsu et al. also reported the highest 
occurrence of IOS in the mandibular fi rst molar region.35
Certain theories were proposed to explain the specific 
location of IOS. For IOS in the molar region, a chronic 
infl ammatory process caused by occlusal forces predisposing the formation of IOS has been proposed.18,36 Another possible explanation for occurrence of IOS lesions 
in the premolar region is that they are induced by remnants of deciduous teeth.13 However, no direct evidence 
demonstrates that either occlusal force or remnants of 
deciduous teeth are associated with IOS. Using computed 
tomography (CT) to examine IOS patients, Yonetsu et al. 
found that some IOS lesions appeared as thickening of 
the cortical bone, and were classifi ed as enostoses while 
those located within the medullary bone were classifi ed 
as central scleroses. Taking these fi ndings together, we 
suggest that IOS represents a normal variants of bone, 
being developmental rather than reactive.35
In conclusion, our data indicate that IOS lesions are 
common in the mandible and prevalent in the premolar 
regions. Although IOS is a well-recognized entity, related information such as its prevalence and distribution 
among DM patients in Taiwan is limited. Our results 
provide additional information regarding IOS in DM 
patients. One important limitation of this study was the 
sample size. Further studies with a larger study population of DM patients would be necessary.
